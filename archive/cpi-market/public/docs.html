The big picture

Used on: every page

An advertiser puts a fixed budget B into a pool. Publishers join the campaign and put content up. Every verified impression they deliver mints one coin. When the campaign closes, every coin is paid the same price, and any budget that wasn't needed goes back to the advertiser.

Nobody sets the price per impression (CPI) by hand. The obvious approach is to split the pool: divide the budget by the coins minted (B / M). That works when supply is plentiful, but it breaks badly when few publishers turn up:

$100,000 budget, only 5,000 impressions delivered. The pool split pays $20 per impression for supply that would happily have taken $3. The advertiser massively overpays for a thin market.

This mechanism fixes that by learning the price across campaigns. Every finished campaign records one fact: "at this posted price, the market delivered this many coins." Pooled together, those facts trace a supply curve, and the next campaign is priced from it.

One campaign alone can't tell whether "no coins" means "the price was too low" or "nobody was around". Hundreds of campaigns together can.

The supply curve & posting a price

How past campaigns become a curve, and how the curve turns into the one price a new campaign posts.

Supply curvePosting the priceWarm-upExplorationEvery "price" explained

The supply curve

Shown on: Live market → Platform supply curve

Every settled campaign adds one observation: the price it posted and the coins it ended up minting. The grey dots on the chart are these observations. The blue line is a smoothed estimate of "how many coins would a campaign get at price p?"

ĝ(p) = Σ coinsⱼ · wⱼ / Σ wⱼ wⱼ = exp( −(ln pⱼ − ln p)² / (2 · 0.18²) )
To estimate supply at a price, take a weighted average of past campaigns, where campaigns priced close to it count most. A campaign priced 20% away gets about 60% weight, and one priced 50% away only about 8%.

It works in log price, so "20% higher" means the same thing at $0.50 as at $5. The curve is evaluated on 61 points spread evenly (in log terms) between the cheapest and the most expensive price ever observed.

The plateau

Once price is high enough, everyone available has already joined and supply stops growing. That flat top is the plateau. It's measured as the median of the curve over its highest-priced fifth, not its maximum, because the maximum of a noisy estimate is biased upward.

Posting the price: the two candidates

Shown on: Live market (dashed lines on the curve, "priced by" pill), both simulators ("posted" pill)

Given a budget B, the curve offers two sensible prices:

CandidateDefinitionMeaning
Budget-exhaust pxcheapest p where p · ĝ(p) ≥ BThe lowest price at which delivery would use up the whole budget.
Saturation pscheapest p where ĝ(p) ≥ 0.95 × plateauThe lowest price that already buys essentially all the supply there is.
posted price = min(p_x, p_s)

The pill shows which one won: budget-exhausted or supply-saturated.

Pay just enough to spend the budget, unless the market runs out of supply first. In that case, pay just enough to buy all of it. Paying more than the saturation price buys nothing extra.
A thin market saturates at 20,000 coins around $3. At $3, 20,000 coins cost only $60k of a $100k budget, so the budget can never be exhausted. ps = $3 wins, and the advertiser gets $40k back instead of paying $5 per coin.

This min is the "healing" rule. Checking px first and ignoring saturation would recreate the pool-split problem.

On the Live market chart, the two dashed markers are computed for a $100,000 budget. A campaign with a different budget can get a different px.

Warm-up: before the curve is trusted

Shown on: Live market ("warm-up n/15"), Market simulator (grey warm-up band)

The curve is only trusted once it has 15 observations. Until then, the price is the advertiser's wanted CPI multiplied by a random factor between e−2 ≈ 0.14× and e2 ≈ 7.4×:

warm-up price = wantedCpi · exp( U[−2, 2] )
During warm-up the advertiser's guess only sets the centre of a very wide sweep. Even if the guess is off by 5×, the sweep still covers the real price, so the guess can't decide where the search looks.

After warm-up the wanted CPI is completely ignored. That's why quoting too high or too low can't steer the price in the long run (see the "Advertiser lowballs / highballs" markets).

The Market simulator additionally leaves the first 40 campaigns out of its score, so the curve has time to settle before it's judged. Scoring →

Exploration after warm-up

If every campaign posted exactly the curve's price, the curve would never learn anything about other prices. So each new campaign is nudged a little:

90% of campaignslocal: p · exp(N(0, 0.1)), roughly ±10% around the learned price
10% of campaignswide: p · exp(U[−2, 2]), anywhere from 0.14× to 7.4×
This is why a campaign-simulator run sometimes posts a strangely high or low price: roughly 1 run in 10 is a deliberate wide exploration. Exploration is the cost of learning a curve nobody can observe directly.

Joining, minting & settlement

The rules while a campaign is live, and how the money is divided when it closes.

SettlementHealed vs fully deployedLifecycle rulesGuarantees

Settlement: one price for every coin

Shown on: Live market (Settle), Campaign simulator (Settlement card), Publisher game (result)

c* = min( posted price p , B / M ) paid D = c* · M (each payout rounded down to the cent) refunded R = B − D
Every coin gets the same price: the posted price, unless there were so many coins that the budget can't cover them all, in which case the budget is split evenly. Whatever's left goes back to the advertiser.
Budget $100,000, posted $2.50.
· 60,000 coins → B/M = $1.67, which is below $2.50 → c* = $1.67. All $100k is paid out, with no refund.
· 10,000 coins → B/M = $10.00, which is above $2.50 → c* = $2.50. $25k is paid and $75k is refunded. The pool split would have paid $10 per coin.

While the campaign is running, the estimated CPI uses the same formula with the coins minted so far. More coins can only push it down, so a publisher's estimate never rises.

After settling, the campaign reports (posted price, coins minted) to the platform curve. That's how the curve learns.

"Healed" vs "budget fully deployed"

healedThe posted price was the binding limit (c* < B/M). Supply was too thin to absorb the budget, so publishers are paid the posted price and the remainder is refunded. The mechanism "healed" the thin market instead of charging the pool split.
fully deployedThe budget was the binding limit (c* = B/M). Supply absorbed everything, so there's no refund.

Lifecycle rules (Live market)

  • Joining is sticky. Content goes up and stays up. A publisher can't re-bid, withdraw or join twice.
  • Minting needs a publisher that has joined and a positive number of impressions. 1 impression = 1 coin.
  • Settling happens once. After that, joins and mints are refused.
  • The duration is stored with the campaign, but the Live market doesn't auto-close. You settle by hand.

Guarantees

  • Budget conservation: paid + refunded = budget, to the cent. Nothing is ever overdrawn.
  • One price per coin, whoever you are and whenever you joined.
  • Never above the pool split: c* ≤ B/M.
  • Nothing to fake: only minting is observed, and payouts are linear in coins, so splitting into many identities gains nothing.
  • Timing-immune: the price is fixed for the campaign. Bursts, trickles and join order don't move it.

These are enforced by the test suite (npm test). What it does not guarantee →

Campaign simulator

What happens when you press "Run campaign", step by step.

The runPublisher marketsSimulated publishersReading the live viewReading the result

What a run does

  1. Seed the randomness. The seed feeds a small deterministic generator. About seeds
  2. Pick the market you chose. If you chose Surprise me, one of the six is picked at random and its numbers are jittered (supply ×e0.45·N, prices ×e0.3·N, publisher count ×e0.5·N).
  3. Build a platform history. A fresh curve is trained on 40 earlier campaigns in the same market, with the same budget, CPI and duration and new publishers each time. This is why the posted price is a learned price rather than your expected CPI.
  4. Post the price from that curve, including normal exploration.
  5. Create this campaign's publishers and run it hour by hour.
  6. Settle with the real settlement code. Settlement →

The hourly join rule

Every hour, each new arrival and each still-patient waiting publisher asks: "if I join now, what will I be paid?"

own = my rate × hours left expected = min( posted price , B / (already committed + own) ) join if expected ≥ my private ask
Publishers join only when the deal still beats their private price after accounting for everyone already in. Early joiners make the pool look more crowded to later arrivals, which is why a crowded market fills up and then stops.

Once in, a publisher mints at their rate every hour until the end. Publishers who decline wait for a while (their patience) and re-check each hour, then leave.

Publisher markets (archetypes)

Markets are defined relative to your own campaign, so "thin" means the same thing whatever budget you type. The target volume is budget ÷ expected CPI.

  • Verdict: healed, fully absorbed, or nobody joined. Definitions
  • Where the budget went: paid vs refunded.

Market simulator

A whole season of campaigns, used to test whether price discovery converges on the truth.

The seasonMarket definitionsThe true priceScoring

What a season does

  1. Start with an empty curve.
  2. For each campaign (a $100,000 budget over 240 hours): generate that campaign's publishers, work out the hidden true price, post a price from the curve, run the campaign with the same hourly join rule, settle, and record the result in the curve.
  3. Score every campaign after the first 40 against its true price.
This is the full learning loop running on fast-forward. Early campaigns are guesses; by campaign 40 or so the teal line should hug the violet one.

Compare all markets runs every market with the same seed and campaign count, giving the same kind of table as REPORT.md (which used 200 campaigns per market).

Each publisher's ask is the median × e0.3·N. Each delivers about 5 coins/hour (varied by e0.5·N) and waits about 72 hours on average before giving up. The lowball and highball markets test that a badly wrong advertiser quote can't steer the price.

The true competitive price

The benchmark everything is scored against. It uses information the mechanism never has: every publisher's private ask.

  1. Line publishers up from the cheapest ask to the most expensive.
  2. Add each one's full potential delivery (rate × hours from their arrival).
  3. Stop where the budget runs out. The price there is the true price. If the budget never runs out, it's the most expensive ask needed to take everyone.
It's what a perfectly informed auctioneer would charge: buy the cheapest supply first until the money is gone.

Publisher game · sim/ladder.js · /api/game/*

The publisher ladder

How the three targets are set, how they move, and why the money is never gamified.

The gameCapability modelThe three rungsHow the bar movesMercy rulesSmooth vs Rungs only

The game

You join one of five campaigns (each priced from a curve warmed on 25 earlier campaigns). Time advances 6 hours per step. Each step you report how many impressions you delivered, and simulated rivals join and mint using the same join rule. At the end, everyone, you included, is settled with the normal settlement.

The ladder shows three cumulative targets (total impressions to reach). The dollar figure under each is your projected payout at that total after dilution by rivals.

Capability model

Targets come from your own recent delivery, not from an outside quota:

  • Centre: a weighted median of your last 8 steps (each older step counts 0.82× the next). Because it's a median, one lucky spike can't drag the bar up.
  • Spread σ: how consistent you are, measured in log terms with a small-sample correction and kept between 0.28 and 0.9.
  • Before any history: the model assumes you deliver what a typical rival delivers in 6 hours.

The three rungs

RungPlacementDesign intent
REACHthe bar itselfCleared about 85% of the time. Deliberately set below typical delivery.
PUSHmax(bar × e1.27σ, 3 × REACH)About a coin flip
STRETCHmax(bar × e2.3σ, 7 × REACH)A real stretch, about 20%

The design follows the "85% rule" and the challenge-point framework (people learn and stay engaged best when they succeed often but not always), goal-setting theory (goals must feel attainable), and flow-channel pacing.

Targets are frozen while you chase them. After a miss, a cumulative target can only move down to meet you, never up.

The 3× / 7× minimum multiples currently override the probability-based spacing for most publishers, so PUSH and STRETCH are harder than their stated odds. See open items.

How the bar moves

  • After a clear: the bar rises toward your demonstrated level (centre × e−1.15σ), at most 4× in one step. If your level has slipped below the bar, it drifts down gently (no more than 5% per step).
  • After a miss: the bar holds for 2 misses, then eases by 12% per further miss.
  • The floor: the bar never goes below the market minimum, which is 30% of what a typical rival delivers per step (at least 5). Below it, the ladder shows "minimum to stay in".
Rising is earned and falling is slow. The floor keeps it a game: a bar that chased a non-delivering publisher down to 1 would reward doing nothing.

Mercy rules & pacing

  • Near miss: delivering within 90% of REACH doesn't break your streak.
  • Streak freeze: one ordinary miss per 5 steps is forgiven.
  • Recovery beat: 3 steps in a row clearing at least PUSH earns a "breather" step where every rung is 22% easier. Difficulty is a sawtooth, not a ramp.
  • Pool nearly full: when reaching a rung would add less than $1 to your projected payout (the pool is effectively yours), rungs stop showing odds and say so.

Smooth vs Rungs only

Smooth shows your real projected earnings. Rungs only is a presentation mode: each step you "bank" the dollar gain of the highest rung you cleared, and the result screen shows it next to the real market settlement.

Money is never gamified. Your actual payout is always coins × settled CPI. Tiers (Bronze 1% → Diamond 30% of the pool) are recognition only.

Random seeds & reproducibility

A seed is a number that fixes every "random" choice in a run. Same seed + same inputs = exactly the same result, which lets you share, compare and debug runs. Seed fields are filled in for you: press 🎲 for a new one, or type your own.

WhereWhat the seed controls
Campaign simulatorEverything: the market pick and jitter, the 40 warm-up campaigns, exploration, and this campaign's publishers.
Market simulatorOnly the mechanism's exploration. Each campaign's publishers are fixed by its position in the season, so every seed faces the same world and you're comparing how the mechanism searched it.
Live market → Add historyNot seeded (it uses the clock), so each click adds fresh history.
Publisher gameOffers are randomly generated each time the lobby refreshes.

The generator is mulberry32, a small, fast, deterministic PRNG. It's fine for simulation, but it isn't cryptographic.

Known limits of the mechanism

  • Exploration costs money. The warm-up plus ~10% off-target campaigns are the price of learning a curve nobody can see.
  • Not strategy-proof. Publishers who hold out for a markup can gain. A uniform price with free entry invites demand reduction.
  • Ask not always met. Uniform price + fixed budget + open entry means that if enough publishers join, B/M can fall below what some of them wanted. Avoiding that needs admission caps or per-publisher prices, both excluded by design.
  • Stable population assumed. A sudden shift in supply is only tracked as new campaigns accumulate.
  • Misquoted thin markets carry extra error from warm-up contamination, and this fades as more campaigns arrive.

Display simplifications (UI only)

  • Campaign simulator publisher table, mid-run: coins are shown as final coins × share of the campaign elapsed, which is a smooth approximation. The final table and settlement use exact figures.
  • Pool-split lines are clipped at the chart's top (the Market simulator scales to the settled and true prices), so a runaway B/M in thin markets doesn't flatten everything else. Hover to read the exact value.
  • Live-market curve markers are computed for a $100,000 budget.
  • Price comparison bars scale to the market prices; a very high pool split simply fills its bar.

Open items for the algorithm polish

Found while writing these docs. Nothing here was changed.

  • 3 ladder tests fail (npm test): "rungs clear at roughly their advertised odds", "the bar eases gradually and stops at the market floor", "a publisher below the market floor cannot simply clear the bar".
  • Rung spacing: the 3× / 7× REACH minimums in ladder() override the σ-based quantiles, which pushes PUSH and STRETCH past their 50% / 20% targets.
  • Rise cap: BAR_RISE_CAP = 4 in code, while the README says 1.5× per step.
  • Headroom fit unused: ladder() computes fit (to scale rungs to what the pool can absorb) but never applies it.
  • Plateau wording: the report says "top price decile", but the code uses the top 20% of the grid.
  • Rungs-only banking lives in the browser only; refreshing the page loses it.
